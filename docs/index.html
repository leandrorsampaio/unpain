Why another finance app?

Because I tried almost all of them.

Some had beautiful mobile apps.

Some automatically synchronized my bank.

Some had great reports.

Every single one eventually sent me back to Excel.

Not because they were bad.

Because they weren't built for someone like me.

So I stopped looking.

and built UnPAIN.

A tour of my own mess

How I actually use it.

No feature grid. Just the screens I open every month, and the specific annoyance each one fixed for me. Click any screenshot to see it full size.

the first thing I open

I don't think in months. I think in years.

Every app I tried dropped me into the current month. But my life doesn't reset on the 1st. So this opens on the whole year: what came in, what went out, what's actually left.

And when a €3,000 bike wrecked my "average month," I built Year costs so one big purchase stops lying to me about the other eleven.

Dashboard
the part I was dreading

I got tired of filing REWE for the 300th time.

I don't mind reviewing a transaction once. I mind doing the same one forever. So new stuff lands in a little queue, I categorize REWE once, hit "apply + rule," and every REWE from here to retirement files itself.

I didn't want to lose the detail. I just wanted to stop repeating it. A few months in, the queue is basically empty and I've stopped thinking about it, which was the whole point.

Review
Rules
the reason it exists, honestly

Splitting everything 50/50 quietly made someone lose.

We earn different amounts, so an even split meant the lower earner was handing over a bigger slice of their income every month. This splits shared costs by what each of us actually earns instead. The monthly figure is a guess; the year-end number is the one we settle on.

And I can print it, so it's a statement we both read line by line, not a number one of us has to take on faith.

Settlement
where I go when something looks off

One Amazon order is never just one thing.

So Amazon and PayPal always land in the queue, where I can split a single order across a few categories or mark part of it out of scope. Then I search, filter, and poke at the list until every cent sits where I actually put it.

And the category tree is mine. I renamed it, nested it, archived the parts I stopped using. Nothing here is hard-coded to someone else's idea of a budget.

Categories
the part I stopped dreading in April

I was rebuilding my tax numbers from scratch every year.

Living in Germany, I'd spend a weekend each spring digging refund-relevant spending back out of a year of transactions. So I made it tag itself into the right buckets all year long instead.

Now when the deadline shows up, the numbers are already sitting there, ready for ELSTER or for a Steuerberater. It's not really the main product, more of a gift to myself, and anyone else in Germany.

How it works

Drop. Review. Done.

Three steps a month. After a few months, step two nearly disappears.

Drop your exports

Download the CSV from each bank and drop the files in one folder. PDF-only statements go through deterministic extractors.

Review the queue

Work through a short queue in the browser. "Apply + rule" teaches the system permanently, so it categorizes itself next time.

Close the month

Check the dashboard, then close the month. Every year carries your rules and categories forward automatically.

Fair, not equal

Equal is not always fair.

When two people earn differently, splitting shared costs 50/50 quietly makes the lower earner pay a bigger share of their income. UnPAIN splits in proportion to income instead: a monthly estimate, and a binding true-up after the year closes.

Alex earns 60%pays 60%
Sam earns 40%pays 40%

Only salary counts toward the ratio. One transfer settles the difference.

Hi, I'm Leandro 👋

I'm a software engineer living in Germany.

I didn't build UnPAIN because I wanted to build a finance app. I built it because I was tired of maintaining a spreadsheet every single month.

For years, I ran my household finances on increasingly elaborate Excel sheets. Eventually one of them got more complicated than the software I write for a living, so I gave it a proper home.

It's open source because I suspect I'm not the only person who enjoys knowing exactly where every cent went. If that sounds familiar… welcome home.
